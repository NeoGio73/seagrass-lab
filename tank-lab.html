Seagrass Tank Lab

Shoal grass (Halodule wrightii) growing in the greenhouse's three tanks: 240, 120 and 80 gallons, under natural light. Treat the water with hydrogen peroxide or nutrients, keep the tanks topped off, and track nitrogen, phosphorus, salinity, dissolved oxygen and magnesium the way the lab does. Then decide what the data can and cannot show.

The plants and water are simulated from published relationships, with illustrative rates. The problems are real ones: tanks differ, transplants die back, water evaporates, walls grow algae, reagents expire and equipment fails.

How the tanks work

The facility. One 240-gallon tank (7 × 4 ft, 15 in tall), one 120-gallon tank (4 × 2 ft, 26 in tall) and one 80-gallon tank in a greenhouse with no lamps. The 80-gallon footprint is assumed to be 4 × 1.5 ft. The greenhouse passes about 70% of outdoor sunlight. Seagrass grows in pots set on the tank floor, so the shallow 240-gallon tank gets the most light at the leaves and the deep 120-gallon tank the least. Containers are 10-liter tubs set inside a tank, which then acts as a water bath.

Each simulated day:

  1. Evaporation lowers the water level and concentrates everything dissolved: salt, magnesium, nutrients. Shallow water loses a larger share of its depth each day, so the 240-gallon tank and the containers drift fastest. Topping off with fresh water restores the salinity; topping off with seawater adds more salt every time.
  2. Hydrogen peroxide breaks down to water and oxygen (each mg/L of H₂O₂ releases 0.47 mg/L of O₂), with a half-life of about a day in clean water and faster with more algae. While it lasts it kills phytoplankton and the film on walls and leaves. Above about 6 mg/L on average for a day it also damages seagrass leaves.
  3. Phytoplankton grow on nitrate and phosphate until one runs out, using roughly 16 parts nitrogen to 1 part phosphorus by moles. Dead algae decompose, which uses oxygen and releases nutrients again.
  4. Oxygen moves toward saturation, about 6.5 mg/L at 28 °C and 30 ppt, quickly with air stones and slowly without. Algae add oxygen by day and use it at night. The DO you measure is a morning reading; the pre-dawn low is lower still.
  5. Seagrass grows with the light reaching its leaves, which falls with water depth, algae and the film on the leaves. It loses shoots in hot water (above about 31 °C), above 40 ppt, when oxygen drops below 2 mg/L at night, and after peroxide burns.

Measurements carry realistic error: nutrient kits about ±10%, refractometer ±0.5 ppt, DO probe ±0.15 mg/L, magnesium kit ±25 mg/L, peroxide strips read to the nearest color block. Magnesium in seawater tracks salinity (about 1,290 mg/L at 35 ppt), so the two should rise and fall together; when they don't, check the reagents.

Dose math. mL of peroxide stock = target mg/L × tank liters ÷ stock mg/mL (about 30 mg/mL for 3%, 396 mg/mL for 35%). Grams of NaNO₃ = target mg N/L × liters ÷ 1,000 ÷ 0.165. Grams of KH₂PO₄ = target mg P/L × liters ÷ 1,000 ÷ 0.228. A kit that reports nitrate as NO₃⁻ reads 4.43 times the nitrate-N value; phosphate as PO₄³⁻ reads 3.07 times phosphate-P.

Where the numbers come from

  • Hydrogen peroxide against brown tide: Randhawa et al. (2012), PLoS ONE. 1.6 mg/L removed a dense brown tide within 24 hours, while diatoms and green algae were suppressed only briefly and rebounded above controls within 72 hours.
  • Seagrass light requirements: Beck, Hagy and Le (2018), Estuaries and Coasts 41:592–610.
  • Indian River Lagoon seagrass loss under blooms: Morris et al. (2022), Frontiers in Marine Science.

The seagrass peroxide threshold, growth rates and event frequencies are illustrative choices, not measurements. Replace them with the lab's own values as the data come in.
