This is a teaching model of a fictional lagoon built from published Indian River Lagoon relationships. It shows how the pieces connect and how fast they respond. It is not a forecast for any real place.

How the model works

Each month the model follows one chain of causes, the same one students draw in the systems-thinking activity.

  1. Nitrogen load. Runoff, septic and stormwater load grows about 1% a year with development. Legacy nitrogen in muck keeps feeding the water and fades slowly. Nutrient projects (Option A) remove nitrogen at about $600 per pound per year, starting a year after funding and phasing in over three years as groundwater flushes.
  2. Algae. Chlorophyll rises steeply with total nitrogen, peaks in summer, and blooms in warm months when algae are already high. Shellfish filter some of it out. Algae control (Option D) cuts it by up to 30% only while funded.
  3. Light. Light fades with depth as e−Kd·z, where Kd comes from water, algae and suspended sediment. Bare bottom stirs up more sediment, so losing seagrass makes the water murkier.
  4. Seagrass. Seagrass needs about 20% of surface light at the bottom. The model finds the deepest point where that is true and the area of lagoon bottom shallower than it. Beds beyond that area die back quickly; beds inside it spread slowly. Planted seagrass (Option B, about $50,000 per acre) survives only when light at 1 m is above the requirement.
  5. Shellfish, fish and the economy. Shellfish grow toward a habitat limit that reef investment (Option C) raises, and they die off when algae are heavy. Fish follow seagrass, shellfish and blooms. The local economy follows water clarity, fish and blooms.

Hurricanes add a pulse of runoff and sediment and tear up some seagrass. Cold snaps knock back fish. Turn random weather off to see the average behavior.

Where the numbers come from

  • Seagrass light requirement: Beck, Hagy and Le (2018), Estuaries and Coasts 41:592–610, which put the Indian River Lagoon median near 18% of surface light; 20% is the commonly cited target.
  • Seagrass loss and slow recovery: Morris et al. (2022), Frontiers in Marine Science, on the 2011–2019 losses and the feedbacks that slowed recovery.
  • Nutrient project costs: Brevard County Save Our Indian River Lagoon cost per pound of nitrogen by project type.
  • Limits of planting: Virnstein (2021), Florida Scientist 84.
  • Shellfish filtration in the lagoon: Galimany et al. (2017), Marine Ecology Progress Series 567.

Coefficients that link nitrogen to chlorophyll and chlorophyll to light were tuned so the model reproduces the handout's 2018 and 2024 values. Treat every output as a relative comparison between plans.
